How Diffusion Models Do Reinforcement Learning: From the Cosmos3 Dual Tower to One Round of FlowGRPO

Created2026-11-16|Updated2026-11-16|Deep Learning
|Word Count:4.5k|Reading Time:28mins|Post Views:

How Diffusion Models Do Reinforcement Learning: From the Cosmos3 Dual Tower to One Round of FlowGRPO

I have been adding text-to-image generation and an RL training recipe for NVIDIA Cosmos3-Nano to the open-source project UniRL. Once the code ran, I noticed there were two things I could not explain: how this “dual-tower” model actually joins text and image, and why a diffusion model can do reinforcement learning at all. This post is the record of me asking until both made sense.

The one sentence I took away: doing RL on a diffusion model means adding a little noise to a few denoising steps, so that “the next latent” becomes an action whose probability you can compute; whether the model is dual-tower or single-tower only decides how text gets read inside that one forward pass.

The post has six parts. It first separates diffusion from transformers, then looks at the Cosmos3 dual tower and the MiniMax-H3 single tower, then explains how added noise gives you a log-prob, then walks through one round of FlowGRPO, and finally covers DiffusionNFT, a route that needs no log-prob. The “Where I got stuck” blocks are questions I actually asked.

The structures and numbers come from the UniRL repository’s code and recipe files, and from transformer_cosmos3.py in diffusers.

Diffusion and transformer are not alternatives

Where I got stuck: H3 produces video and audio together. Is it diffusion or a transformer? I know images have diffusion models and transformer models.

It is both. The two words answer different questions. Diffusion (which includes flow matching) is about how you generate: start from noise and denoise step by step. Transformer is about which network you use: the neural network that, at every denoising step, computes which way to move.

The two axes combine freely. Stable Diffusion 1.5 is diffusion with a convolutional U-Net. SD3, Flux, the Cosmos3 generation tower and H3 are all diffusion with a transformer. Models that cut an image into discrete tokens and predict them one by one are autoregression with a transformer. What I had thought of as “the diffusion camp and the transformer camp” is really one kind of network paired with two generation methods.

Both models in this post use flow matching. The convention is

\[ x_t = (1-\sigma)\,x_0 + \sigma\,\varepsilon \]

where \(x_0\) is the clean latent, \(\varepsilon\) is Gaussian noise, and \(\sigma\) goes from 1 down to 0. The model predicts the velocity \(v = \varepsilon - x_0\). Sampling lowers \(\sigma\) to 0 over a number of steps, moving a little each time: \(x \leftarrow x + v\,dt\), with \(dt = \sigma_{\text{next}} - \sigma < 0\). This is an Euler step.

How text enters the model: the Cosmos3 dual tower

Cosmos3-Nano has about 16B parameters and is a Mixture-of-Transformers (MoT): an understanding tower of about 8B reads the text, and a generation tower of about 7B predicts the velocity. There is no separate text encoder.

The design is easier to see next to two other structures.

The two models in this post are (b) and (c). (a) is the common text-to-image structure and is here as a reference: H3’s text path is the same as (a), and the difference is that its backbone handles video tokens and audio tokens together.

The test for a tower is whether, inside one layer, different modalities use different backbone weights. In each Cosmos3 layer, Cosmos3VLTextMoTDecoderLayer, text goes through to_q / to_k / to_v / to_out and mlp, while image goes through add_q_proj / add_k_proj / add_v_proj / to_add_out and mlp_moe_gen. So there are two towers.

How shapes change in one forward pass

Take a single 832×480 image. The VAE is the one from Wan2.2, with 48 channels and 16× spatial compression, so the latent is [48, 1, 30, 52].

The “causal” label on the understanding tower in the figure is the same rule in training and in inference. The prompt is given in full, so one forward pass computes all text positions in parallel, but each text token still sees only the text before it.

Where the numbers come from:

  • 390 image tokens: the 30×52 grid is merged 2×2 into 15×26 = 390 patches. Each patch concatenates the 48 channels of 4 positions, giving 192 dimensions, which proj_in maps to 4096.
  • The timestep is added once, at the input: \(\sigma \times 1000\) goes through time_embedder and the resulting vector is added to the noisy image tokens. Text tokens do not get it.
  • The output mirrors the input: proj_out maps 4096 dimensions back to 192, and the patches are reassembled into [48, 1, 30, 52], which is the velocity \(v\).

The 36 layers, hidden size 4096, 32 query heads, 8 key-value heads and vocabulary of 151936 are the default arguments of this class in diffusers. The parameter count I estimate from them matches the size of Nano, but I did not confirm them by reading the checkpoint’s config.

Attention runs twice per layer

“The generation tower can see the understanding tower, but not the reverse” is not implemented with one large attention mask. The layer simply computes attention twice:

  • First pass: the text Q, K and V all come from text, with causal attention, so each text token sees only the text before it.
  • Second pass: Q comes from the image (add_q_proj), and K and V are the text ones and the image ones concatenated. Image tokens see all of the text and all of the image.

The text K and V borrowed in the second pass are computed by the understanding tower’s own to_k / to_v. The generation tower has no second set of weights for processing text.

Where I got stuck: During generation, are the text tokens given all at once? What about during training?

Yes, in both cases. Tokenization happens once before generation starts and produces two fixed token strings, for the positive and the negative prompt. Every forward pass after that packs the whole text string and the current image latent into one sequence. The understanding tower is used here only as a reader and never generates word by word. The causal mask is structure and autoregressive sampling is a way of using it; only the first is present here. Training is the same: the SFT loss is computed only on the noisy image tokens, and RL replay uses the two token strings stored at sampling time. In the diffusers forward, lm_head exists but is not called.

Where I got stuck: Then why connect at every layer? Compute the text once and concatenate it in on every forward pass.

It depends on what “concatenate once” concatenates. Structure (a) also puts text in the sequence, but what it puts there is the encoder’s last-layer output, which the diffusion model then reprocesses with its own weights. The dual tower lets layer \(\ell\) of the generation tower read the representation at layer \(\ell\) of the understanding tower directly, so both the literal information in shallow layers and the semantic information in deep layers are available; the generation tower’s parameters handle only the image; and the understanding tower’s weights are left untouched, so it remains a complete language model.

My intuition was half right. Text cannot see the image and carries no timestep, so every layer of the understanding tower gives exactly the same result across the 16 denoising steps. In principle it could be computed once and stored, although what you would store is the K and V of every layer rather than one layer’s output. In the current code, every forward pass recomputes the understanding tower from layer 1 to layer 36.

In short: what the dual tower means for training is simple. The understanding tower is frozen. LoRA goes only on the generation tower’s attention, that is add_q_proj / add_k_proj / add_v_proj / to_add_out.

The single-tower H3, and other multimodal routes

MiniMax-H3 is a different structure: text in, video plus stereo audio out, 33B parameters. Its text is encoded into vectors by a separate Qwen3-VL, and those vectors then enter the same diffusion transformer together with the video and audio tokens.

It works in four steps. Video and audio each have a VAE that compresses them into a string of vectors. The three kinds of tokens are concatenated into one sequence, each token carrying “which modality I am” and “where I am in time and space”. One transformer runs self-attention over the whole sequence and outputs a video velocity and an audio velocity. Both sides take one step, and this repeats for 10 steps.

Sound and picture line up because in the third step video tokens and audio tokens can see each other. The two go from blurry to sharp together, so the picture of a door closing and the sound of it land at the same moment.

Where I got stuck: Can I think of H3 as three towers?

No. It is one tower with three doors. Each H3 block has one attention and one feed-forward layer, and all three kinds of tokens go through the same weights. Only three things differ by modality, and all are thin: one linear layer each at the input, one linear layer each at the output, and the scale and shift after each layer’s normalization, which are looked up by “timestep × modality”.

The two models side by side:

Cosmos3-Nano MiniMax-H3
Parameters About 16B (understanding about 8B, generation about 7B) 33B, plus a separate text encoder
Structure Dual tower, two sets of weights per layer Single tower, one set of weights per layer
How text enters Tokens go straight into the understanding tower, and the generation tower reads its K and V at every layer Qwen3-VL encodes it into vectors, which are concatenated into the sequence
Output of each forward pass Velocity of the image latent One velocity each for the video latent and the audio latent
Final product Image (this post uses a single frame) Video plus stereo audio
How the timestep is injected Added once at the input Modulation after each layer’s normalization, by “timestep × modality”
Can it output text The understanding tower has an lm_head, unused on the text-to-image path No
Where LoRA goes Generation-tower attention only The shared attention and feed-forward layers

The last row follows directly from the structural difference. Cosmos3 lets you pick only generation-tower layers to train. H3 has no such split, so once LoRA is attached, the processing of text, video and audio tokens all changes.

Where I got stuck: Is all multimodal training done this way, so the model can output both text and images?

No, the dual tower is only one route. Others: a language model writes a prompt and hands it to an independent text-to-image model; images are also cut into discrete tokens and put in the same vocabulary as text, so everything is next-token prediction (Chameleon); one transformer does next-token prediction at text positions and diffusion at image positions (Transfusion). They all answer the same question: text is discrete and images are continuous, so how do you put both in one model.

Also, Cosmos3’s two abilities were not trained together here. The text ability comes from the understanding tower, which is frozen during training; the image ability comes from the generation tower. H3 has no text output at all.

Where I got stuck: Does the training cost for audio-video depend entirely on the number of frames?

More precisely it depends on the total number of tokens, and the frame count is one factor in that product. But for H3 the frame count is not a freely adjustable knob: the VAE also compresses in time, so 124 pixel frames become 37 latent frames; the frame rate is fixed at 24 fps, and 124 frames is the smallest valid value. What the recipe actually uses to save memory is resolution: replay costs about 2.2 GB per step at 256×384 and about 12 GB at 768×768.

Why deterministic sampling cannot do policy gradient

Policy gradient needs to know “how probable was this action”, which is the log-prob. In an Euler step, given the current latent, the next latent is uniquely determined, so there is no probability to speak of.

Deterministic sampling does not produce the same image every time: the starting noise is drawn at random, and a different draw gives a different image. But the distribution of the starting noise does not depend on the model’s parameters, so it provides no gradient. Also, “cannot” here applies only to policy gradient, which needs a log-prob; DiffusionNFT, covered later, samples deterministically.

The fix is to add a little Gaussian noise to the result at a few of the steps.

Now “the next latent” follows a Gaussian distribution, and the model’s output sets its mean. In RL vocabulary:

RL Here
State Current latent \(x\), step index, prompt
Action Next latent \(x_{\text{next}}\)
Policy Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \text{std}^2)\), with \(\mu\) set by the model with LoRA
Trajectory The whole 16-step denoising chain
Reward Given only at the end: decode to an image and score it

Mean and variance in FlowSDE

The Cosmos3 recipe uses FlowSDEStrategy. With \(s = \eta\sqrt{\sigma/(1-\sigma)}\), the mean in the code simplifies to

\[ \mu = x\left(1 + \frac{\eta^2\,dt}{2(1-\sigma)}\right) + v\left(1 + \frac{\eta^2}{2}\right)dt \]

\[ \text{std} = s\,\sqrt{-dt}, \qquad x_{\text{next}} = \mu + \text{std}\cdot z,\quad z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I) \]

\(\eta\) controls the size of the noise. With \(\eta = 0\) the mean falls back to \(x + v\,dt\) and the standard deviation is 0, which is the Euler step. So the same class takes both kinds of step, and the code switches with eta if index in sde_set else 0.0.

A worked example. At step 8, \(\sigma = 0.75\), the next value is \(\sigma_{\text{next}} = 0.70\), \(dt = -0.05\) and \(\eta = 0.7\). Then \(s = 0.7\sqrt{3} \approx 1.21\), \(\text{std} \approx 1.21 \times \sqrt{0.05} \approx 0.27\), and the mean is \(0.951\,x - 0.062\,v\).

The log-prob is the Gaussian log density, per element

\[ \log p = -\frac{(x_{\text{next}} - \mu)^2}{2\,\text{std}^2} - \log \text{std} - \tfrac{1}{2}\log 2\pi \]

Two details are easy to miss. First, the code takes the mean over all latent dimensions rather than the sum, so the values are small, which is why clip_range ends up on the order of 1e-3. Second, \(x_{\text{next}}\) in this expression is .detach()ed, so the gradient flows only through \(\mu\), which means only through the model output \(v\).

CPS: another way to add noise

The H3 recipe switches to CPSSDEStrategy. The reason given in its comments is that FlowSDE at \(\eta = 0.7\) leaves visible noise in the picture.

FlowSDE adds noise on top of the existing step. CPS (coefficients-preserving sampling) first estimates the clean-image component and the noise component from the current state:

\[ \hat{x}_0 = x - \sigma v, \qquad \hat{\varepsilon} = x + (1-\sigma)\,v \]

It then shrinks the estimated noise component and tops it up with fresh noise:

\[ \mu = (1-\sigma_{\text{next}})\,\hat{x}_0 + \sigma_{\text{next}}\cos\!\left(\tfrac{\eta\pi}{2}\right)\hat{\varepsilon}, \qquad \text{std} = \sigma_{\text{next}}\sin\!\left(\tfrac{\eta\pi}{2}\right) \]

Because \(\cos^2 + \sin^2 = 1\), the old and new noise together have a coefficient of exactly \(\sigma_{\text{next}}\), matching the noise level the schedule prescribes. Its log-prob in the code is also simpler: just \(-(x_{\text{next}} - \mu)^2\), without dividing by the variance.

Where CFG comes in

Where I got stuck: Cosmos3 has a cond and an uncond forward pass. Do models generally have CFG?

Most do, but not all. CFG (classifier-free guidance) replaces the prompt at random for part of training, so the same model learns both to “draw what the prompt says” and to “draw anything”; at sampling time you compute both and extrapolate: \(v = v_{\text{uncond}} + g\,(v_{\text{cond}} - v_{\text{uncond}})\). The cost is two forward passes per step. Distilled models have this effect trained into a single forward pass and no longer need it. In H3’s RL recipe guidance_scale is 1.0, so each step runs once.

The Cosmos3 recipe uses \(g = 4.0\), and its “unconditional” branch uses a negative prompt rather than an empty one. The positive and negative prompts have different token lengths, and the model takes only one sequence per call, so the two forward passes run one after the other.

For RL, the \(v\) in the formulas above is the velocity after guidance. Both forward passes carry gradients during replay, so what gets optimized is “the guided policy”, the same one used at sampling time.

One round of FlowGRPO

Now to put the pieces together. The Cosmos3-Nano recipe is: 16 denoising steps, 2 of them SDE steps, 16 images per prompt, PickScore as the reward, and only LoRA (rank 64) on the generation-tower attention is trained.

Sampling: 2 steps picked out of 16

Why train only 2 steps: every replayed step runs the model with gradients, so fewer steps cost less. As for why the middle of the schedule, the repository only says it follows an existing recipe. My reading is that when \(\sigma\) is close to 1, \(\sigma/(1-\sigma)\) is large and the noise is too strong; and noise added in the last few steps has no time to be removed, so the image comes out dirty.

Inside one SDE step:

All of sampling runs under no_grad. Only the latents before and after the two chosen steps are stored.

Scoring and comparing within a group

Subtracting the group mean removes the effect of “how easy this prompt is to draw”. One image has one advantage value, shared by the two stored SDE steps of its trajectory.

Replay and the loss

Sampling had no gradients, so the stored \((x, x_{\text{next}})\) pairs are fed to the model again with one question: under the current model, what is the probability of going from \(x\) to this \(x_{\text{next}}\)?

\[ \text{ratio} = \exp(\log p_{\text{new}} - \log p_{\text{old}}), \qquad L = \max\big(-A\cdot\text{ratio},\; -A\cdot\text{clip}(\text{ratio},\,1-c,\,1+c)\big) \]

\(A\) is the advantage and \(c\) is clip_range. If the advantage is positive, the mean \(\mu\) moves a little toward the \(x_{\text{next}}\) that was actually reached; if negative, it moves the other way.

The count per sample: sampling is 16 steps × 2 forward passes = 32; replay is 2 steps × 2 (positive and negative prompt) × 2 (old and new) = 8. That makes 40. One round is 48 × 16 = 768 samples.

Clipping does nothing in this recipe

I only confirmed this after reading the training loop. The order within a round is: replay with the current weights to get the old log-prob, then compute the loss sample by sample and accumulate gradients, and step the optimizer once for the whole round (num_updates_per_batch: 1).

When the new log-prob is computed, the weights have not changed yet, so the ratio is always 1 and never leaves the clipping interval. The gradient of the loss is then \(-A\,\nabla \log p\), plain policy gradient with a group baseline. The code even has a check watching for this: if the ratio deviates from 1 before the first update, it prints a warning.

clip_range only constrains the step when a batch is updated on more than once.

One algorithm, two recipes

H3’s first recipe also uses FlowGRPO, with the same algorithm code. The differences are all in the parameters:

Cosmos3-Nano MiniMax-H3
Canvas 832×480, single frame 256×384, 124 frames plus audio
Denoising steps 16 10
Noise scheme FlowSDE CPS
CFG 4.0, two forward passes per step 1.0, one per step
Size of a round 48 prompts × 16 8 prompts × 16
Reward PickScore ImageBind + CLAP
LoRA Rank 64, generation-tower attention Rank 32, attention and feed-forward
clip_range 1e-3 5e-3

The H3 reward has a trap, recorded in the recipe’s comments: if ImageBind compares only audio and video, the model learns to produce a black screen with silence to game the score, so the text-to-video term has to be included.

Another route: DiffusionNFT

H3 has a second recipe, which uses DiffusionNFT. It needs no log-prob, so sampling can be fully deterministic (\(\eta = 0\)).

It takes the clean latent \(x_0\) from sampling, re-noises it to some level \(t\), and has two models each predict the velocity: \(v_{\text{new}}\) from the one being trained, and \(v_{\text{old}}\) from a moving average of older weights. From these it builds a positive and a negative prediction:

\[ v^{+} = v_{\text{old}} + \beta\,(v_{\text{new}} - v_{\text{old}}), \qquad v^{-} = v_{\text{old}} - \beta\,(v_{\text{new}} - v_{\text{old}}) \]

The positive prediction is a small step from the old model toward the new one, and the negative prediction is the same step in the opposite direction. Each prediction recovers a clean image, \(\hat{x}_0 = x_t - t\,v\).

The loss is

\[ L = r\,\lVert \hat{x}_0^{+} - x_0 \rVert^2 + (1-r)\,\lVert \hat{x}_0^{-} - x_0 \rVert^2 \]

where \(r \in [0, 1]\) is derived from the advantage: the advantage is divided by 5, clamped to \([-1, 1]\), and mapped linearly to \([0, 1]\). In the code both terms are also divided by \(\beta\) and can be weighted adaptively by the size of the error; I leave that out here.

For a good sample \(r\) is close to 1 and the first term dominates, pulling the new model toward this image. For a bad sample \(r\) is close to 0 and the second term dominates: it makes the “opposite-direction” prediction fit this image, which amounts to pushing the new model away from it.

Compared with FlowGRPO, it does not store intermediate steps and does not keep a full computation graph with gradients for replay. That is why H3’s NFT recipe can train all 10 timesteps.

Recap

  1. Diffusion is a generation method and the transformer is a network architecture; the two combine. Cosmos3 and H3 are both diffusion with a transformer.
  2. To count towers, check whether different modalities use different backbone weights inside one layer. Cosmos3 has two, H3 has one.
  3. In one Cosmos3 layer attention runs twice: text sees only text; image sees text plus image, where the text K and V are borrowed from the understanding tower.
  4. In inference, SFT and RL replay alike, the text is a condition given in full. Only the image part is learned and sampled.
  5. Deterministic sampling has no probability. Add Gaussian noise to a few steps and the next latent becomes one action of a Gaussian policy, which gives the log-prob a definition.
  6. One round of FlowGRPO is: sample, score, subtract the group mean to get advantages, replay to recompute the log-prob, update the LoRA.
  7. With one update per batch the ratio is always 1, PPO-style clipping does nothing, and training is equivalent to plain policy gradient.
  8. DiffusionNFT needs no log-prob and instead uses a re-noise-and-retrain loss that pulls toward good samples and pushes away from bad ones.

What I have not figured out

  • The understanding tower’s K and V do not change across the 16 steps. I have not measured how much computation caching them would save.
  • In H3’s two recipes, one comment says FlowGRPO trains all 10 steps and another says it could only afford 4. They disagree, and I have not tracked down which one is out of date.
  • This post does not derive the FlowSDE mean from the SDE, and I have not worked through that derivation myself yet.
